# How pre-modern close combat was really fought - what historians know

Research note for `trax_combat_enhancements`, written 2026-10-25. Question from Toni: do real men
walk up and swing until someone dies, or do they dance around, keep their guard, fight in
pulses with lulls, and have to be driven to close again? What do historians actually know,
medieval first, with ancient and early-modern evidence beside it?

**How to read this.** Every important claim carries its source and a grade:

- **well-established** - several independent lines of evidence agree, little serious dispute;
- **plausible** - the best current reading, but resting on thin or indirect evidence;
- **disputed** - serious scholars disagree;
- **speculative** - an inference or a single weak source.

"Checked" means I read the passage this session (the article text, the primary-source
translation, or a scholarly summary that quotes it). "Not checked" means I know the work exists
and what it is usually cited for, but did not read the passage today. Battle numbers from
chroniclers are always suspect; the grades already allow for that.

---

## Summary

1. **Close combat between steady infantry was not continuous.** When neither side broke at the
   first shock, infantry fights could last one to several hours (Roman sources, checked; Hastings
   ran about nine hours, which contemporaries and modern historians call unusual). Nobody can
   swing a weapon for hours. The model most scholars of Roman battle now favour (Sabin 2000,
   Goldsworthy 1996, Zhmodikov 2000) is a **stand-off a few metres apart, broken by short local
   surges into contact, then a step back to "safety distance"**. One ancient eyewitness-based
   account (Appian on Forum Gallorum) describes exactly that: men drawing apart to catch breath
   "as in gymnastic games", then closing again. *Plausible, close to a consensus for Rome; for
   the Middle Ages the same picture fits the evidence but has been argued less directly.*
2. **Losses were lopsided: victors lost little, losers lost a lot, and most of the gap opened
   when one side broke.** Hoplite victors lost about 5 %, losers about 14 % (Krentz 1985); Roman
   victors usually 5 % or less, while losers often lost half their army killed or captured
   (Sabin 2000, from Polybius, Livy, Caesar). *Well-established as a pattern; "most deaths in the
   rout" is the best explanation but not the only one - sometimes men who could not flee were
   killed where they stood (Cannae).*
3. **The hard part was getting men to close, not to keep them fighting once in reach.** Du Picq
   (1860s, checked) argued the clash is mostly moral: one side usually gives way before or at
   contact, and real bayonet fights were "extraordinarily rare". Musket-era studies (Griffith,
   Muir) point the same way. *Well-established for the musket era; plausible by analogy for
   earlier periods.* The modern "most men will not kill" claim (Marshall 1947, Grossman 1995) is
   **weak**: Marshall's ratio of fire appears to have been invented (Spiller 1988, checked) and
   other wartime evidence contradicts it (Engen 2008, checked).
4. **Fresh men mattered more than deep files.** The Romans relieved tired front units with fresh
   ones from behind (Livy, Caesar, checked via Sabin). A constant man-by-man rotation inside each
   file is **not** supported by the sources (Sabin). Officers and natural fighters started the
   surges; when they fell, the surge died (Sabin; du Picq). *Line relief: well-established;
   rank rotation: disputed, probably not routine.*
5. **In the press men fought cautiously, behind the shield, at weapon's length.** Most strikes
   that killed went to the head, arms and legs - the parts the shield and armour did not cover
   (Visby 1361, Towton 1461, Sidon 13th c.). Being packed too tight was deadly: it stopped men
   using weapons or stepping back, and in the worst cases they were crushed or suffocated under
   their own fallen (Agincourt 1415, Dupplin Moor 1332). *Well-established from graves and
   chronicles; exact percentages are weaker than often quoted.*

## 1. How long did close combat last, and did it come in pulses?

### Rome: long fights that cannot have been continuous

- **Battles of hours.** Livy describes several battles lasting hours; Plutarch says Pydna was
  unusually quick at one hour; Vegetius says battles were usually settled in two or three hours;
  Caesar says an isolated fight of several cohorts at Ilerda went on for five hours (BC 1.45-47).
  The "internal clock" is the strongest proof: at Cannae, the Metaurus and the Sambre, the
  cavalry or a reserve made long flank marches while the infantry centre fought on without
  decision. Source: Philip Sabin, "The Face of Roman Battle", *Journal of Roman Studies* 90
  (2000) 1-17, section III (checked, full text). **Well-established** that Roman infantry fights
  could last an hour or more; Sabin notes we are far *less* sure of this for Greek hoplites.
- **Some fights ended at the first shock.** Sabin and Goldsworthy both stress that many clashes
  were decided immediately, when one side broke at or before contact (Allia, Herdonea in Livy;
  Goldsworthy argues these quick decisions were commoner for late-Republican professionals).
  **Well-established.**
- **Neither a "rugby scrum" nor continuous sword-duelling fits.** Sabin's argument: an hour of
  shield-to-shield shoving with short swords inches from faces, or an hour of unbroken duelling
  along a mile-long front, would kill far more of the *victors* than the ~5 % the sources give.
  His own back-of-envelope (note 45): even with only the front rank fighting, one blow every
  five seconds and under 1 % of blows fatal, each army would lose 5 % every ten minutes.
  **Plausible** - a strong argument from numbers, not direct evidence.
- **The model: stand-off plus surges.** Sabin's conclusion (p. 14-16): the "default state" of a
  prolonged fight was the two lines a small distance apart, exchanging missiles and insults but
  not quite in sword reach. Junior leaders and the boldest men periodically led local surges into
  contact; each flurry lasted until one side got the worst of it and stepped back to the safety
  distance, brandishing weapons to deter pursuit. If one side kept giving ground after each
  flurry, the line moved back over hundreds of yards without routing. He compares it to boxers
  circling more than they exchange blows. **Plausible**; Sabin himself calls it an "emerging
  consensus" of the few scholars who studied it, and expects a challenge.
- **The other two models he cites.** Adrian Goldsworthy, *The Roman Army at War 100 BC-AD 200*
  (1996), ch. 5: drawn-out melees were punctuated by **lulls**, when both sides drew back to
  breathe and replace wounded front-rankers - argued mainly on practical grounds (continuous
  duelling is physically unendurable). Alexander Zhmodikov, "Roman Republican heavy infantrymen
  in battle", *Historia* 49 (2000) 67-78: pila and other missiles were used throughout long
  fights, not just in one volley before the charge (at Ilerda the pila ran out only after five
  hours). Both summarised in Sabin (checked); the books themselves not checked. **Plausible.**
- **The best single ancient passage.** Appian, *Civil Wars* 3.68 (Forum Gallorum, 43 BC), two
  veteran legions fighting each other: they raised no battle-cry; when a man fell he was at once
  carried off and another took his place; and when fatigue overcame them they **drew apart for a
  brief space to take breath, "as in gymnastic games", and then rushed again to the encounter**.
  Checked in the Loeb/White translation on Perseus. Appian wrote some 150 years later, probably
  from an eyewitness letter (Galba's, preserved in Cicero). **Plausible-to-strong** as a
  description of one fight; it is a single passage, so it cannot by itself prove the pattern was
  universal.
- **Missile stand-offs before contact.** Livy 9.35 (310 BC): the Etruscans threw away their
  missiles to close with swords, then found it "difficult" to actually come to close quarters,
  and stood under a hail of Roman javelins and stones until disordered (via Sabin, checked).
  **Plausible** (Livy on the 4th century BC is a weak source; Sabin says so).

### Greece: the othismos ("push") debate

- **Orthodox view** (Victor Davis Hanson, *The Western Way of War*, 1989; also in Hanson ed.,
  *Hoplites*, 1991): hoplite battle was short and brutal - a run into contact and a literal mass
  shove (*othismos*) until one side broke. Not checked today; this is how all parties summarise
  his position.
- **Heterodox view** (Peter Krentz, "The nature of hoplite battle", *Classical Antiquity* 4
  (1985) 50-61; Hans van Wees, *Greek Warfare: Myths and Realities*, 2004): fighting was looser
  and more individual; men came forward and fell back; "push" in the sources is largely a
  metaphor. Van Wees in particular disputes battles as "massive pile ups" of physical shoving
  (StrategyPage review). Not checked in the books.
- **State of play:** *Men of Bronze: Hoplite Warfare in Ancient Greece* (ed. Kagan & Viggiano,
  Princeton 2013) gathers both camps; the publisher says the contributors "sharpened their
  differences" rather than agreed. **Disputed - unresolved.** Note the hoplite case may genuinely
  differ from the Roman one (big round shield, deep files, flight easier because shields were
  thrown away).

### Medieval: long fights are the exception, but they have the same shape

- **Most battles were short; Hastings was not.** Stephen Morillo ("Hastings: an unusual
  battle", repr. medievalists.net, checked) notes it is hard to find more than a handful of
  ancient or medieval battles lasting more than an hour or two; Tinchebrai (1106) was decided in
  about an hour. Hastings lasted from "the third hour of the day until dusk" - at least nine
  hours. **Well-established.**
- **What filled those nine hours** was not continuous slaughter. William of Poitiers (via
  Morillo) describes a phase where "one side launched attacks and numerous manoeuvres, the other
  stood like rocks fixed to the ground": repeated Norman attacks, mounted and on foot, against a
  shield wall that held, with **feigned flights** drawing parts of the English line out to be
  ridden down. Morillo sees "no reason to doubt" the feigned flights. **Well-established** that
  the battle came in repeated attacks rather than one long melee; the feigned flights are
  **plausible** (some historians have doubted them; Morillo accepts them).
- **Agincourt (1415)**, as reconstructed by John Keegan, *The Face of Battle* (1976): the
  two lines of men-at-arms ended up divided "at a distance of ten or fifteen feet" by a fence of
  waving and stabbing spear-shafts (quoted in Sabin 2000, checked). That is a stand-off at
  weapon's length, not an intermingled brawl. Sabin points out how closely Keegan's picture
  matches his Roman model. **Plausible**; Keegan's reconstruction is influential but is a
  reconstruction.

## 2. Medieval evidence: how men fought in the press

### What the graves show

- **Visby, Gotland (1361).** Three mass graves, about 1,185 individuals, excavated 1905-1928
  (Thordeman, *Armour from the Battle of Wisby*, 1939; injury study by Bo Ingelmark). Ingelmark
  recorded 456 cut wounds (sword, axe) and 126 arrow/bolt wounds; **most injuries were in the
  lower half of the body**, which the excavators put down to shields covering the upper body.
  Head-wound rates differed by grave (42 %, 52 %, 5 % - the last group perhaps had helmets).
  Source: medievalists.net summary "Medieval battle injuries" (2024, checked) and the Swedish
  History Museum page. **Well-established** that leg wounds were very common. The often-quoted
  figure of "about 70 % of blows to the lower leg" comes from a popular book and is called a
  rough estimate there - **weak**; I could not trace it to Thordeman. Visby was a massacre of a
  poorly equipped peasant levy by professionals, so it shows how armoured men cut down
  less-armoured ones, not how two equal lines fought.
- **Towton (1461).** A grave of roughly 37-38 men found at Towton Hall in 1996 (counts differ
  between accounts; one summary gives 51). Published as V. Fiorato, A. Boylston, C. Knüsel (eds),
  *Blood Red Roses* (Oxbow 2000); the injury chapter is by Shannon Novak. Findings (via
  medievalists.net 2024 and a JAMA summary, checked; the book not read): almost every skull had
  battle wounds, many men had several (one had nine on the body, Towton 25 had eight to the
  head); no torso wounds (armour); the commonest body wounds were to the **forearms, consistent
  with parrying**; many had old healed wounds - veterans. Novak read the many blows after
  incapacitation as a frenzied killing. Whether these men died in the fight or were overtaken in
  the rout is **disputed**: face wounds suggest some faced their killers; the absence of arrow
  wounds and back-of-head blows suggest others were caught fleeing. **Well-established:** heads
  and arms were the targets of armoured men; **disputed:** which phase killed them.
- **Sidon (mid-13th c.).** Mikulski et al., "Weapon injuries in the crusader mass graves...
  Sidon", *PLoS ONE* 16(8) (2021) e0256517 (checked): at least 25 men; more wounds on the
  **back** than the front, which the authors read as men struck while running; many more blows
  to head, neck and shoulders than to the legs (mounted attackers); hand wounds (warding off
  blows); cuts to the back of the neck suggesting decapitation of captives. **Plausible** for
  this one event - it is a defeat and probably a pursuit, not a line fight.

**What the graves can and cannot tell us.** Graves hold the *losers*, and they cannot tell the
clash from the rout from executions afterwards. Their safest lesson is about *where* blows
landed: wherever the shield and armour did not cover - legs (Visby), forearms and head (Towton),
back and neck when men ran (Sidon).

### What the chronicles and historians show

- **Crowding killed.** At **Agincourt** the French men-at-arms advanced into a narrowing,
  muddy field. Keegan argues the rear ranks barged forward, knocking front-rankers off their feet
  and taking away their room to step back, pressing them onto English weapons (via Sabin,
  checked). The *Gesta Henrici Quinti* (an English priest's eyewitness account) says the dead
  lay in heaps higher than a man in places (reported in Osprey's "Agincourt 600" and other
  summaries; I did not read the Latin text). A crowd-dynamics model (Clements & Hughes 2004) found
  a few large mounds rather than a continuous wall of dead plausible. **Well-established** that
  crowding and falling were central; how literal the "heaps" were is **disputed**.
- **Dupplin Moor (1332).** English archers shot into the flanks of the Scottish schiltrons; men
  pressed inward for shelter, the rear kept coming, and many were suffocated or trampled. The
  Lanercost Chronicle says the pile of dead was higher than a spear's length
  (medievalists.net 2024, checked). **Well-established** as an event; the numbers are not.
- **Knights rarely killed knights.** Orderic Vitalis on Brémule (1119): about 900 knights
  fought and he was told "only three were killed" - because they were in mail, spared each other
  "out of fear of God and fellowship in arms", and wanted to capture rather than kill
  (De Re Militari translation, checked). Matthew Strickland, *War and Chivalry* (1996) builds on
  this kind of evidence (not checked today). **Well-established** for 12th-century
  knight-on-knight fights; it changed when infantry who took no ransoms entered (Courtrai 1302,
  the Swiss, the Wars of the Roses).
- **Infantry that stood beat knights.** Kelly DeVries, *Infantry Warfare in the Early Fourteenth
  Century* (1996), and J.F. Verbruggen's work on Courtrai, argue that foot soldiers who kept
  their formation and their nerve on good ground repeatedly defeated cavalry charges. Not checked
  today; standard in the field. **Well-established.**
- **Clifford Rogers** (e.g. *Soldiers' Lives through History: The Middle Ages*, 2007) writes on
  the soldier's experience including the aftermath of battle; I could not verify a specific
  claim of his on casualties, so I cite nothing from him.

### Swiss and landsknecht pike: "push of pike"

- **The textbook picture:** two pike blocks level pikes and close until bodies meet, then push
  until one gives way; the rear ranks add weight; the front ranks die impaled or crushed
  (Wikipedia "Push of pike", citing Oman and others; checked). Sometimes both sides paused to
  rest by agreement (same page, unsourced there). **Plausible** for some fights.
- **Against it:** Stuart Peachey, *The Mechanics of Infantry Combat in the First English Civil
  War* (1992), cited by Sabin: contemporary accounts suggest real pike fights were tentative,
  cautious "fencing" at pike length rather than "mutual kebabs" (Sabin p. 9, checked; Peachey not
  read). Sabin links the Hellenistic lengthening of the sarissa to the same logic: outreach your
  enemy in a fencing contest. **Disputed.**
- **For violent contact sometimes:** Blaise de Monluc on Ceresole (1544) says that when the
  columns met, all the first ranks on both sides were overturned, by push of pike or by the
  shock, and the second and third ranks won the day; arquebusiers were hidden behind the first
  rank of pikes and held fire until touching (old English translation via warhistory.org,
  checked second-hand). Monluc boasted, and others credit the cavalry. **Plausible** that some
  pike fights were short and murderous at the front - the two pictures can both be true:
  long tentative fencing, or a sudden lethal collision when both sides really meant it.

## 3. Where did most casualties happen - in the clash or in the rout?

- **Asymmetric losses are well attested.** Krentz ("Casualties in hoplite battles", *GRBS* 26,
  1985): hoplite victors lost on average about 5 %, losers about 14 %. Roman battles: victors
  around 5 % in the Punic Wars, even less later (Cynoscephalae 700, Magnesia 350, Pydna 100,
  Pharsalus 230 dead), while losers often lost over half killed or captured (Zama 20,000 dead and
  nearly as many taken). All via Sabin 2000 section III (checked). Sabin warns the victors'
  figures are flattering. **Well-established as a pattern**, even allowing for inflation.
- **Why: two ends of a spectrum.** Sabin: either losses during the fighting were one-sided (the
  loser could not reply - Cannae, men encircled and packed), or both sides lost little until the
  rout and the loser died in the flight. Different battles sit anywhere between. In long
  fights the clash was sometimes fairly even: Ilerda, five hours, Caesar 70 dead and 600
  wounded vs about 200 Pompeian dead. **Well-established** that the decisive killing usually
  came when one side lost cohesion; **plausible, not proven,** that "most deaths were in the
  pursuit" as a general law.
- **Du Picq said it plainly** (*Battle Studies*, Part One; checked in the 1921 translation):
  in ancient battle the victors lost but few and the vanquished were exterminated, and that is
  what held men in place - turning your back meant death. He also notes flight began in the
  rear ranks. **Plausible** - a 19th-century soldier's reading of ancient sources, still widely
  accepted.
- **Pursuit depended on who could catch whom.** Hoplites who threw away shields often
  outran heavy pursuers ("tearless battles"); Roman and medieval losers were ridden down by
  cavalry (Sabin; Plutarch on Pydna; Caesar on Ariovistus; Hastings' feigned flights; Sidon back
  wounds). **Well-established.**
- **Medieval knights were often captured, not killed** (Brémule above) - so "rout" in a
  12th-century knightly fight meant prisoners, while in an infantry battle with no ransom
  culture (Courtrai, Towton, Visby) it meant massacre. **Well-established.**

## 4. The psychology: reluctance to close and to kill

- **Ardant du Picq, *Battle Studies*** (written 1860s, published 1880; English 1921; checked on
  Project Gutenberg #7294). Core claims: man enters battle not to fight but to win, and avoids
  the first to get the second; discipline keeps enemies face to face "a little longer" but cannot
  remove fear; a body that really waits for a bayonet charge to bayonet distance is
  "something extraordinarily rare"; in practice one side "makes an about" at some stage of the
  advance, "perhaps only at two paces"; "the shock is a mere term". He scorns the idea that the
  victor is the one who kills most. **Well-established** as an influential, experience-based
  view; it is an argument, not data.
- **Musket era.** Sabin (2000, p. 13, checked) summarises: bayonets caused only a tiny share of
  18th-19th-century casualties, yet bayonet charges triggered routs; if one side nerved itself to
  charge believing the enemy would not stand, the enemy usually broke before contact; if neither
  did, the fight bogged down into a close-range firefight lasting hours. He cites Paddy Griffith,
  *Forward into Battle* (2nd ed. 1990) ch. 2, who argued that British infantry beat the French
  with one volley and a charge rather than long firefights. **Well-established** that bayonet
  wounds were rare; **plausible** that charges were decided morally before contact.
  Rory Muir, *Tactics and the Experience of Battle in the Age of Napoleon* (Yale 1998) and Brent
  Nosworthy, *Battle Tactics of Napoleon and his Enemies* (1995) are the standard works on this
  and are usually cited for the same picture (charges that rarely met, the moral contest decided
  by steadiness and the will to advance), but **I could not check their specific passages
  this session** - treat this attribution as unverified.
- **S.L.A. Marshall's "ratio of fire"** (*Men Against Fire*, 1947): only 15-25 % of US
  riflemen fired in combat. **Weak / discredited as data.** Roger Spiller, "S.L.A. Marshall and
  the Ratio of Fire", *RUSI Journal* (Winter 1988) 63-71 (checked): Marshall's assistant never
  recalled him asking who fired; his notebooks show no statistics; the "systematic collection of
  data" "appears to have been an invention". Spiller still credits Marshall with real insight.
- **Dave Grossman, *On Killing*** (1995): humans have an innate resistance to killing their own
  kind; distance makes killing easier. **Disputed / weak in its strong form.** Robert Engen,
  "Killing for Their Country: A New Look at 'Killology'", *Canadian Military Journal* 9(2), 2008
  (checked): the biology does not support an innate block (chimpanzees kill each other); Marshall
  was "simply wrong"; Canadian WWII battle questionnaires (several hundred) complain of troops
  firing *too much*, not too little. Engen *accepts* that Grossman's material on fear, heart rate,
  and distance is valuable. Also John Whiteclay Chambers II, *Parameters* (2003) - cited by
  Engen; not read.
- **What survives the critiques.** Reluctance to *close* (fear for oneself) is far better
  supported than reluctance to *kill*. The ancient and medieval evidence - stand-offs, missile
  duels, men backing away to safety distance, flight beginning from the rear - fits fear and
  self-preservation; it does not need Grossman's innate block. Note Goldsworthy's estimate that
  three-quarters of Roman front-rankers fought mainly to stay alive was explicitly modelled on
  Marshall (Sabin p. 11), so it is weaker now than in 1996. **Plausible** (the behaviour) /
  **weak** (the numbers).

## 5. Rotation, relief, fatigue, and who renews the fight

- **Line relief by whole units: well-established for Rome.** The multiple-line system (hastati /
  principes / triarii; later the cohort triplex acies) existed mainly so fresh troops could
  replace or reinforce tired ones (Livy 8.8; Caesar BC 1.45-46 at Ilerda and 3.94 at Pharsalus;
  ancient writers repeatedly credit fresh-vs-tired as a Roman edge - Livy 9.32, 34.14-15;
  Onasander 22). Via Sabin, checked. Du Picq (checked): the best dispositions assured "the relief
  by ranks of units in action" and kept the rest outside "the immediate sphere of moral tension";
  rear ranks waiting under strain sometimes gave way before their turn came.
- **Man-by-man rotation inside a file: disputed, probably not routine.** Sabin (p. 11): no clear
  ancient evidence for a constant queue in each file, and arguments against it - the Greeks put
  their best men in front and rear, centurions fought continuously in the front rank (hence their
  high losses). What *is* attested: wounded front-rankers carried off and replaced by the man
  behind (Appian BC 3.68, 4.128), and Caesar's note that Galba's camp was too thinly held to
  replace wounded or weary men while the Gauls could (BG 3.4-5). A forum-reported point that the
  Byzantine *Strategikon* puts better-armoured file leaders at the front points the same way
  (not checked).
- **How long can a man fight?** No ancient source gives a figure. A "15 minutes of hand-to-hand"
  figure circulates, attributed to Goldsworthy or Fuller, but I could only find it second-hand on
  a forum - **speculative**. Hard data on armour: Askew, Formenti & Minetti, "Limitations imposed
  by wearing armour on Medieval soldiers' locomotor performance", *Proc. R. Soc. B* 279 (2012)
  640-644 (abstract checked): walking in 15th-century plate (30-50 kg) cost 2.1-2.3 times the
  energy of walking unloaded, running 1.9 times, worse than the same weight carried on the
  trunk, partly because the breastplate restricts breathing. **Well-established** (lab
  measurement on modern re-enactors). Hastings' nine hours with repeated attacks, and Appian's
  breathers, both imply that fighting in bursts was a physical necessity.
- **Who renews the push.** Sabin: the surges were started by "natural fighters" and junior
  leaders (centurions, standard-bearers who threw the standard into the enemy to shame men
  forward - Plutarch Aem. 20; Caesar BG 4.25); when those leaders were killed or exhausted, the
  sides separated again. Morillo on Hastings: William held his army together by personal example,
  and Harold's survival kept the English line in place; the end came with his death. Du Picq: the
  Roman edge was discipline plus officers who kept supports at the right distance.
  **Plausible-to-well-established**: leadership as the trigger of renewed attacks is a constant
  in the sources, but it is hard to measure.
- **Breaking starts from the back.** Caesar at the Sambre (BG 2.25) saw men slipping away from
  the rear of hard-pressed cohorts; Napoleonic columns broke from the back; du Picq says the same
  of ancient battle (Sabin p. 4; du Picq, checked). **Well-established.**

## 6. What men physically did in the press

- **Guard first, strike rarely.** Goldsworthy's picture (quoted in Sabin p. 11-12): most men
  fought very cautiously, getting the most from their shields, watching their opponents and
  only occasionally delivering a weaker blow while exposing as little of their sword arm as they
  could; a minority fought far more aggressively, shield-barging and cutting hard. **Plausible**
  (part of his reasoning leaned on Marshall).
- **Space to use the weapon mattered.** Caesar at the Sambre (BG 2.25) ordered the Twelfth
  Legion to open ranks because the men were too packed to use their swords (via Sabin, checked).
  Real "too tight to strike" scrums (Livy 34.46; Ammianus 18.8, dead held upright by the crowd)
  were exceptional, in narrow places. **Well-established.**
- **The safety distance and the danger of being pinned.** Sabin's key point: front-rankers could
  shy back from the enemy without bumping into the man behind; when that "give" was lost - by
  encirclement (Cannae) or by rear ranks pushing forward (Agincourt) - men could neither fight
  properly nor step back, and were slaughtered. **Plausible-to-well-established**, supported by
  Agincourt and Dupplin Moor.
- **Falling was deadly.** Agincourt and Dupplin Moor: men who went down in mud or the crush were
  trampled, smothered, or killed while down (chronicles above). Towton: many blows landed after
  a man was already incapacitated (Novak). **Well-established.**
- **Aim where the guard is not.** Legs below the shield (Visby), forearms raised to parry
  (Towton), head, neck and back on men who turned (Sidon, Towton). **Well-established** as a
  pattern from bones.
- **Missiles throughout.** Rear ranks kept throwing javelins over the front during long fights
  (Plutarch on 2nd Chaeronea; the Mainz relief; Zhmodikov via Sabin); at Hastings arrows were
  used repeatedly. **Plausible** for Rome, well attested at Hastings.
- **Feints and fencing.** Direct descriptions of feints between individuals in a battle line
  are thin. Pike "fencing" (Peachey via Sabin) and Keegan's "fence of waving and stabbing spear
  shafts" are the closest. Feigned *retreats* by whole units (Hastings) are much better attested
  than individual feints. **Speculative** at the man-to-man level.

## What this suggests for the game

Plain design ideas, each tied to the findings above. They describe a feel, not an implementation.

1. **Fight in pulses with a stand-off between them.** When two steady infantry lines meet and
   nobody breaks at first contact, they should settle at a few metres apart (just out of weapon
   reach) and stay there, then surge into contact in short local flurries, then step back again.
   *Sabin 2000, Goldsworthy 1996, Appian 3.68, Keegan on Agincourt (sections 1 and 6).*
2. **In contact, guard much more than strike.** Most men in reach hold the shield/block and
   only throw an occasional, safe strike; a few aggressive men attack hard. Aggression should be
   a trait of the few, not the default. *Goldsworthy via Sabin; du Picq (sections 4, 6).*
3. **Let tired and wounded front men step back and fresh men step in** - and make it cleaner
   between whole units than within a file. A unit with fresh men behind it should outlast a
   deeper unit packed in one block. *Line relief (Livy, Caesar, du Picq), section 5.*
4. **The hard part is to close again.** After a lull, a line should need a trigger to surge -
   an officer, a hero, a banner, a fresh unit arriving, or a visibly wavering enemy. Without it,
   the stand-off continues. *Sabin's "junior leaders", standard-bearers, Morillo on William
   (section 5); du Picq on charges (section 4).*
5. **Kill a leader, end the surge.** When the man who led the surge falls, the men around him
   should step back to the stand-off. *Sabin p. 16; Hastings, Harold's death ending the
   battle (section 5).*
6. **Most deaths in the rout.** Keep losses in the stand-off and the flurries low, and let the
   real killing happen when a side breaks and runs - especially with cavalry pursuing.
   Victors around 5 %, losers far more. *Krentz, Sabin, du Picq, Sidon (section 3).*
7. **Breaking starts at the back.** Morale collapse should show first as men slipping away
   from the rear ranks, not the front. *Caesar BG 2.25, du Picq (section 5).*
8. **Crowding is dangerous.** A unit squeezed from behind or from the flanks should lose its
   ability to step back and to swing, and take heavy losses; men knocked down in the press
   should be in great danger. *Agincourt, Dupplin Moor, Cannae, Sambre (sections 2, 6).*
9. **Charges are decided by nerve.** A determined, ordered charge should often break a shaky
   line before contact; if both sides are steady, the charge should stall into the stand-off
   rather than become a bloodbath. *Du Picq, Griffith via Sabin (section 4).*
10. **Fatigue drives the rhythm.** Stamina should drain fast in contact (and faster in heavy
    armour), and the lull is where it comes back. That alone produces pulses. *Askew et al. 2012;
    Appian's breathers; Hastings' nine hours of repeated attacks (sections 1, 5).*
